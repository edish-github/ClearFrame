# Clearance practice notes

*Team-authored working notes for the ClearFrame grounding corpus. Original prose
summarising publicly documented clearance practice. Not legal advice, and not a
substitute for a licensed network or studio clearance handbook.*

## Two chains for every music use

A song in a picture is two properties: the **master recording** — the specific
recorded performance, usually controlled by a label — and the **underlying
composition** — the written work, controlled by publishers, songwriters, or their
estates. Both must be licensed for a synchronisation use. Licensing one clears
nothing on its own.

A **cover recording** almost always separates the two chains: the master belongs
to whoever recorded the cover, and the composition remains with the original
writers' publishing. A finding that resolves both chains to the same party on a
cover is more likely an error than a discovery.

Compositions change hands. Catalog acquisitions, administration deals, and
reversion after a writer's death all move control, and databases lag. A publisher
of record named in a registry entry is a lead, not an answer; the current
administrator is the party who can grant the licence.

## Prominence and the limits of "background"

Prominence changes exposure but rarely removes it.

- **Hero** — the element is the point of the shot, or the story turns on it.
  Always license, or replace.
- **Featured** — a viewer would notice it, name it, or read it. Treat as
  licensable exposure.
- **Background** — incidental and unemphasised. Lower exposure, but never zero,
  and never "cleared" by virtue of being small.

Two things defeat a background argument reliably: **duration** (a logo held on
screen reads as a placement) and **framing** (anything centred, focused, or lit is
not incidental).

## Artwork, murals, and the living artist

Visual art in frame is a copyright question about someone else's work. The single
fact that most changes the exposure is whether the **artist is living or their
estate is active**: a living artist's work used without licence is a claim
waiting to be made, whatever the framing.

Murals and street art are not public property because they are in public. Tattoos
are original works fixed in a medium and have supported claims. Posters and album
sleeves in a set-dressed room are the classic "background" trap.

An **orphan work** — a work whose rights holder cannot be identified or located —
is an unresolved chain, not a free one. Record the search that was made; that
record is what a diligent-search defence would rest on.

## Trademarks in picture

A visible mark is usually a **depiction** question rather than a licensing one:
the risks are consumer confusion, implied endorsement, dilution, and — often more
practically — an owner with a history of objecting.

Escalate when: the mark appears in a context the owner would object to (illegal
conduct, disparagement, adult content); the depiction implies sponsorship; the
mark is altered; or the owner has a documented record of pursuing depictions.

Enforcement temperament is an evidentiary question. Either there is a record of
this owner acting on depictions or there is not; company size is not evidence.

## Real people

Depicting or naming a real person raises publicity rights, defamation, and false
light — and the exposure varies sharply by jurisdiction of exploitation. Some
jurisdictions recognise post-mortem publicity rights for decades; others recognise
none.

Two flags escalate immediately: a depiction that could be read as **false or
damaging**, and a subject or estate with a **documented history of objecting**.
A private individual depicted identifiably is a higher exposure than a public
figure discussed in a public capacity.

## Archival and stock footage

Provenance is the whole question. An agency's willingness to license a clip is not
proof of the agency's rights, and a public-domain claim needs a stated basis and a
stated jurisdiction — publication year, formality failure, term expiry — not an
assertion.

Watch for: footage licensed from an aggregator that itself sourced it from a third
party; news footage carrying separate rights in the underlying events, music, or
appearances; and collections subject to a later provenance correction.

## Fonts and typefaces

The typeface design and the font software are treated differently across
jurisdictions, and font licences frequently distinguish desktop, broadcast, and
merchandising use. Titles and prominently featured signage are the uses that get
noticed.

## What "resolved" means

An item is resolved when its chain of title is established and the use is
licensed, altered, replaced, removed, or documented as defensible. An item whose
ownership could not be established is **not** resolved, and should never be
reported as cleared. The honest state is "unresolved chain — exposure is the
not-knowing", and it is at minimum an amber.
